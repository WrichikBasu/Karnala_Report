	\section{Karnala Bird Sanctuary}
	
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{karnala_entrance.jpg}

	The Karnala Bird Sanctuary lies on NH~66, in the Panvel taluka of Raigad (erstwhile Raigarh) district of Maharashtra, some \SI{54}{km} from Mumbai. Centred on the Karnala Fort, it occupies an area of \SI{12.11}{sq. km}.\\
	
	The Survey of India covers this area in topographic sheet \texttt{47F/1}, also designated Open Series Map \texttt{E43H1}. The tract has been demarcated as a Reserve Forest, although current official records classify it under IUCN category~IV (habitat/species management area). The computed coordinates of the officially demarcated entrance to the sanctuary are $\ang{18;53;41.604}N$, $\ang{73;6;54.0822}E$, while the ruins of the Karnala Fort stand at $\ang{18;52;55.5744}N$, $\ang{73;7;0.9144}E$. These positions, together with the contours of the region, are shown in figure~\ref{fig:topo}.\\
	
	Though small in area, the sanctuary is home to more than 222 species of birds, of which 161 are resident. The following species endemic to the Western Ghats have been recorded here:%

		\item \ifnt{Treron affinis} (grey-fronted green pigeon);
		
		\item \ifnt{Columba elphinstonii} (Nilgiri woodpigeon);
		
		\item \ifnt{Psittacula columboides} (Malabar blue-winged parakeet);
		
		\item \ifnt{Ocyceros griseus} (Malabar grey hornbill);
		
		\item \ifnt{Megalaima viridis} (white-cheeked barbet);
		
		\item \ifnt{Galerida malabarica} (Malabar lark);
		
		\item \ifnt{Leptocoma minima} (small sunbird); and 
		
		\item \ifnt{Aethopyga vigorsii} (Vigors's sunbird).
		
	\end{enumerate}
	
	The sanctuary has further been designated an Important Bird Area (IBA), satisfying both the A1 (globally threatened species) and A2 (restricted-range species) criteria. It also supports 114 species of butterflies.

		\subsection{Objectives of visit}
		
		\begin{itemize}
			
			\item \bfnt{Biodiversity and ecosystem study:} The sanctuary is a tract of moist deciduous and semi-\hspace{0pt}evergreen forest in the foothills of the Western Ghats. It therefore allows students to observe how a forest ecosystem functions as a whole: the interdependence of flora and fauna, the structure of food chains and food webs, and the role of habitat in sustaining species.
			
			\item \bfnt{Ornithological study:} As Karnala is known primarily as a bird sanctuary, the central purpose of the visit is the observation of avian diversity in a natural setting. Students learn the field identification of resident and migratory species, study bird behaviour, calls, feeding patterns and nesting, and come to appreciate the difference between observing birds in the wild and reading about them.
			
			\item \bfnt{Understanding conservation and protected areas:} A sanctuary is a legally protected area, and a visit therefore conveys the purpose and functioning of such zones, the threats they face --- habitat loss, human encroachment and poaching --- and the reasons why conservation matters. It connects classroom concepts of environmental protection to a real and actively managed site.
			
			\item \bfnt{Field methodology and observation skills:} An excursion of this kind trains students in the practical side of science: recording observations, using binoculars and field guides, maintaining a field notebook, sketching or photographing specimens, and cultivating the patience and discipline that data collection demands.
			
			\item \bfnt{Ecology and environmental awareness:} Direct exposure to a natural habitat builds sensitivity towards environmental issues and towards the value of the Western Ghats as a biodiversity hotspot, reinforcing the concepts of sustainability and of human impact on ecosystems.
			
		\end{itemize}

		On the walls of an adjoining enclosure we observed \bfnt{\ifnt{Adiantum}}, the maidenhair fern. Under the older classification these were grouped among the pteridophytes, though Pteridophyta is no longer accepted as a valid taxon. Modern classification instead proceeds Embryophyta\ \textrightarrow\ Tracheophyta, the latter subdividing into two groups:%
%		
		\begin{enumerate}
			
			\item Lycopodiophyta; and
			
			\item Euphyllophyta, which further splits into:%
%			
			\begin{enumerate}[label={(\alph*)}]
				
				\item Polypodiophyta, under which \ifnt{Adiantum} comes, and
				
				\item Spermatophyta, which includes all seed plants.
				
			\end{enumerate}
			
		\end{enumerate}
		
		Following PPG~I (2016), the full classification runs as follows:\\
		
		Eukarya\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Archaeplastida\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Viridiplantae\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Streptophyta\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Embryophyta\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Tracheophyta\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Euphyllophyta\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Polypodiophyta\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Polypodiidae\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Polypodiales\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Pteridaceae\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ Vittarioideae\ \ \textrightarrow\ \ \ifnt{Adiantum sp.}
		
		\begin{center}
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fern_moss.jpg}
			\captionof{figure}{\ifnt{Adiantum sp.}, along with moss on the wall.}
		\end{center}
		
		A small rock nearby bore crustose lichen, seen in figure~\ref{fig:lichen}. Lichens merit a brief digression at this point. A lichen is not a taxon in its own right, for it is not a single organism but a symbiotic phenotype formed by an alga and a fungus. The fungus derives nutrition from dead and decaying matter, while the alga photosynthesises from whatever sunlight is available. Under the botanical code, a lichen takes its name from its fungal partner. Lecanoromycetes is one large class of lichen-forming fungi, though by no means the only one; there are even lichens in which the photosynthetic partner is not a eukaryote at all, but a cyanobacterium.\\
		
		On the basis of growth form, lichens may be divided into the following categories:%
%		
		\begin{enumerate}
			
			\item \bfnt{Crustose:} thallus fused to the substrate with no lower cortex, cannot be removed intact. E.g. \ifnt{Lecanora}, \ifnt{Graphis}, \ifnt{Rhizocarpon}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Foliose:} leaf-like and dorsiventral, with upper and lower cortex, attached by rhizines. E.g. \ifnt{Parmelia}, \ifnt{Xanthoria}, and \ifnt{Peltigera}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Fruticose:} shrubby or pendulous, radially symmetrical in section. E.g. \ifnt{Usnea}, \ifnt{Cladonia}, \ifnt{Ramalina}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Squamulose:} small overlapping scales or squamules, loosely attached at one edge, intermediate between crustose and foliose. E.g. \ifnt{Cladonia} basal thallus, \ifnt{Psora}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Gelatinous:} homoiomerous, always with a cyanobacterial photobiont, usually \ifnt{Nostoc}. Dry and brittle when dehydrated, swelling into a dark jelly when wet. E.g. \ifnt{Collema}, \ifnt{Leptogium}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Filamentous:} fungal hyphae wrapped around filaments of the photobiont, producing a hair-like or felty thallus. E.g. \ifnt{Ephebe}, \ifnt{Cystocoleus}, \ifnt{Racodium}.
			
			\item \bfnt{Byssoid:} wispy in texture, resembling teased wool.
			
			\item \bfnt{Foliicolous:} growing on living leaves, common in tropical humid forests including the Western Ghats.
			
		\end{enumerate}

		Climbing the hill beyond the rain-fed spring, over slippery rock and mud on which our shoes struggled to find purchase, we came upon a \ifnt{Gnetum} tree. It is an advanced gymnosperm, and in several respects the closest of the gymnosperms to the modern angiosperms. Unlike most gymnosperms, which bear needle-shaped leaves, it has broad leaves resembling those of an angiosperm, and it has long been regarded as an evolutionary connecting link between the two groups.\\
		
		\ifnt{Gnetum} confounded botanists for the better part of a century, for it carries a set of angiosperm-like characters found in no other gymnosperm:
		
		\begin{itemize}
			
			\item Broad, flat leaves with reticulate venation, borne in opposite and decussate pairs, closely resembling the leaf of a dicot;
			
			\item Vessel elements in the xylem, absent from all other gymnosperms except \ifnt{Ephedra} and \ifnt{Welwitschia};
			
			\item A form of double fertilisation, in which the second male gamete fuses with a further nucleus, although no endosperm is produced;
			
			\item Strobili sometimes described as flower-like, with a perianth-like envelope around the ovule;
			
			\item The absence of archegonia, which is unusual among gymnosperms.
			
		\end{itemize}
		
		This combination gave rise to the anthophyte hypothesis, which placed the Gnetales as sister group to the angiosperms. Molecular evidence has since overturned that view: the Gnetales now resolve within or alongside the conifers, and the angiosperm-like characters are recognised as convergent, the reticulate venation and the vessel elements having evolved independently. The case remains one of the clearest textbook illustrations of morphological convergence misleading classification for decades.

	\section{Yusuf Meherally Center}
		
		The Yusuf Meherally Center is situated at Tara village, near Karnala, in Panvel taluka of Raigad district, Maharashtra, about \SI{18}{km} from Panvel in Navi Mumbai, on the Mumbai--Goa road. It is a voluntary organisation registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860 and the Bombay Public Trust Act, 1950.\\
		
		The Center is named in memory of Yusuf Meherally (1903--1950), a socialist freedom fighter who was elected Mayor of Bombay in 1942 while imprisoned in Yerawada Central Prison, and who founded the National Militia, the Bombay Youth League and the Congress Socialist Party. He is best remembered for coining two of the independence movement's most famous slogans: ``Simon Go Back'', raised in 1928 in protest against the all-British Simon Commission, and ``Quit India'', coined on \nth{14} July 1942 and approved by Gandhi at Wardha. Imprisoned eight times during the freedom struggle, he died in 1950 at the age of forty-seven. The Center was established by his associates so that his name and his ideals would not be forgotten. One of its co-founders, G.~G.~Parikh, put the matter plainly: ``We wanted to ensure that Yusuf Meherally's name is not forgotten. Many people volunteered to join and start the centre. Back then the purpose of the centre was to work towards `national integration' and to bring people together across divisions.''\\
		
		Over time its mission sharpened from ``national integration'' into rural development, guided by a particular diagnosis of India's problems. As Parikh described it, the Center came to concentrate on the problems arising from rapid urbanisation in Mumbai, and concluded that their solutions lie not in the cities but in the hinterland: rural areas must be developed and provided with basic amenities so that people remain there, and no one should be compelled to leave their village merely because no alternative exists. The founding philosophy holds that the development of India means rural development, that the right to work is a fundamental right, and that free and compulsory education, together with community health care and medical aid, should be available to all alike.\\
		
		The Center runs a cluster of village industries at Tara, most of them established decades ago, which convert local raw materials into everyday products while generating non-farm employment. The dated timeline in YMC's own records shows how these were built up: the Ghani Oil Industry began in 1978, the Soap Unit in 1986, the Bakery in 1987 and Vermiculture in 1994. The principal units are:%
%		
		\begin{itemize}
			
			\item Tel Ghani (traditional oil pressing);
			
			\item Handmade soap unit;
			
			\item Pottery;
			
			\item Vermiculture and vermi-composting; and
			
			\item Carpentry, from recycled wood (mostly teak).
			
		\end{itemize}
		
		Alongside the manufacturing units lie agricultural and organic-farming operations --- paddy and vegetable cultivation, and a nursery of medicinal plants --- together with demonstrations of biogas energy.\\
		
		The products are sold both on site and in wider markets. Pottery, handmade soaps and oils are all available at the Center, which has in addition learned to market not merely its own goods but those of other village industries as well.\\
		
		What raises these units above the level of a craft shop is the deliberate development philosophy behind them. The Center rejects the Western industrial model outright; as Parikh argued, that model is an energy-guzzling machine and is not sustainable. In its place, YMC defines rural development by a specific formula, drawn from nearly two and a half decades of experience: micro-watershed development, together with organic farming, non-conventional energy, and village industries and the marketing of their products.\\
		
		The case for decentralised small industry is set out in the Center's own model. Advances in technology have made the decentralised production of goods possible, and, given the rising costs of pollution and of ecological destruction, small-scale production has become not merely viable but necessary. The Center argues further that the generation of employment is preferable to doles, and that the country may be developed by developing the countryside, since doing so creates considerable wealth and a large number of jobs, raising rural incomes and stimulating the wider economy.\\
		
		The industries are one arm of a far broader effort to make village life viable, so that people are not driven to migrate to the city slums. The full range of YMC's activities encompasses rural development, health care, education, the empowerment of women and Adivasis, youth mobilisation, employment generation, organic farming and vermiculture, and relief and rehabilitation. \\
		
		In concrete terms, the Center assists the surrounding population through:
		
		\begin{itemize}
			
			\item \bfnt{Employment generation:} The village industries and organic farms provide landless labourers, small farmers and tribal (Adivasi) families with paid, local, non-farm work, so that no one need leave their village for want of an alternative.
			
			\item \bfnt{Health care:} Health services are among the Center's core activities. YMC operates regular dispensaries, mobile clinics and eye camps, and has built a thirty-bed cottage hospital, extending medical aid to communities that would otherwise have little access to it.
			
			\item \bfnt{Education and vocational training:} The Center started the Yusuf Meherally High School at Tara in 1990, and in 1995 opened a Non-Formal Vocational Center for Adivasis, teaching such skills as automobile repair, electrical wiring and maintenance. It also runs a multi-skill residential training programme, the DBRT, covering agriculture; engineering (fabrication, construction and carpentry); energy and environment (electrical work, motor rewinding, surveying, solar power and biogas); home and health (sewing and food processing); and animal husbandry (poultry, goat farming and dairy). These programmes transmit the very skills that the industries employ, so that training and livelihood reinforce each other.
			
			\item \bfnt{Watershed and ecological restoration:} The Center's technical cell promotes micro-watershed development across the Konkan districts, and YMC has run such programmes in Thane, Raigad, Sindhudurg and Ratnagiri. The stated benefits are that soil erosion is arrested and productivity raised, that the scarcity of drinking water, fodder, fuel and timber is reduced, and that need-based emigration to urban areas declines.
			
			\item \bfnt{Women's empowerment and disaster relief:} The empowerment of women and Adivasis runs through the village industries and the agricultural training alike, and the Center has an established record in relief and rehabilitation following disasters.
			
		\end{itemize}
		
		In short, the Yusuf Meherally Center was founded to honour a freedom fighter by carrying forward his conviction that the real development of India lies in its villages. Its sustainable small industries are the practical expression of that conviction: low-energy, local-material, employment-generating enterprises which, together with the Center's work in health, education, watershed management and training, seek to make rural life prosperous enough for people to stay and thrive where they are, rather than migrate to overcrowded cities.
